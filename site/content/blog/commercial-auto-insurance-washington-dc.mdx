A personal auto policy may not be designed for vehicles owned by a business or used regularly for commercial work. Commercial auto insurance can address eligible liability and physical-damage risks associated with business vehicles, but the correct policy depends on who owns the vehicle, who drives it, and how it is used.

Washington, DC businesses range from contractors and consultants to delivery operations, nonprofit organizations, retailers, and professional services. Each presents a different combination of vehicles, drivers, routes, equipment, and liability exposure.

## When a business may need commercial auto coverage

Commercial coverage may be appropriate when a company owns or leases a vehicle, employees drive for work, vehicles carry tools or products, or the operation transports people or property for compensation.

The business name on a vehicle title, a commercial registration, specialized equipment, frequent job-site travel, delivery activity, and higher liability requirements are all reasons to review the arrangement with an insurance professional.

Occasional commuting is different from driving between clients, hauling materials, or making deliveries throughout the workday. Describe the actual use rather than relying on a broad label such as “service” or “business.”

## Core parts of a commercial auto policy

Available protections can include:

- **Auto liability** for eligible bodily injury or property-damage claims arising from covered vehicles.
- **Collision coverage** for eligible damage caused by impact or overturn.
- **Comprehensive coverage** for eligible non-collision events such as theft, vandalism, or certain weather losses.
- **Uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage**, subject to state rules and policy terms.
- **Medical or personal injury protections** when required or selected.
- **Towing, rental reimbursement, or other optional coverages** when available.

Limits, deductibles, exclusions, and covered-auto symbols matter. The policy must identify which autos are covered for each protection.

## Hired and non-owned autos

A company can face auto liability even when it does not own a vehicle. Employees may use personal cars for errands, deliveries, client visits, or travel between job sites. A business may also rent or borrow vehicles.

Hired and non-owned auto coverage may address certain liability exposures involving rented vehicles or employee-owned vehicles used for company business. It generally does not replace the vehicle owner's insurance or automatically provide physical-damage coverage for every rented vehicle.

Discuss expense reimbursement, driver policies, rental agreements, and how often employees use personal vehicles for work.

## Drivers and vehicle information

Insurers commonly review each driver's license status, experience, accident history, and role. They may also consider vehicle type, value, garaging location, radius of operation, annual mileage, overnight parking, and the materials or passengers carried.

Prepare accurate driver and vehicle schedules. Update the insurer when vehicles are added or removed and when employees begin or stop driving. Allowing an unreported or ineligible driver to operate a company vehicle can create serious problems.

## Special operations need special review

Trucking, passenger transportation, food delivery, towing, hazardous materials, and vehicles with permanently attached equipment can require specialized underwriting or filings. A standard commercial auto market may not fit these operations.

Contractors should discuss trailers, mobile equipment, tools, and materials. Physical damage to the vehicle does not automatically insure everything stored in or attached to it. General liability, inland marine, cargo, workers compensation, and umbrella coverage may need to coordinate with the auto program.

## Information to gather before requesting quotes

Have the following ready:

1. Legal business name, address, entity type, and years in operation.
2. Description of operations and the exact vehicle use.
3. Vehicle identification numbers, values, ownership, and garaging locations.
4. Driver names, license information, and employment roles.
5. Typical and maximum driving radius.
6. Prior insurance, current limits, and loss runs when available.
7. Contracts or certificates that specify required limits.
8. Details about trailers, equipment, deliveries, passengers, or regulated activity.

Complete information helps avoid comparisons that look inexpensive only because an important exposure was omitted.
