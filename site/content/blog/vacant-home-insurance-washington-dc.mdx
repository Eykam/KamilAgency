A home can become vacant during a renovation, estate settlement, sale, tenant transition, or extended move. Whatever the reason, vacancy changes the risk. An empty property may be more vulnerable to unnoticed water damage, theft, vandalism, fire, and delayed emergency response.

A standard homeowners or landlord policy may limit or exclude certain losses after a property has been vacant for a defined period. The definition and timing vary by policy, so owners should review coverage before—not after—the occupancy changes.

## Vacant is not always the same as unoccupied

Insurance policies may treat “vacant” and “unoccupied” differently. A furnished home whose owner is away temporarily is not necessarily viewed the same as a house with no residents, limited furniture, and disconnected utilities.

Tell the agent exactly what is happening at the property. Important details include whether furniture remains, utilities are active, someone checks the home, renovation work is underway, or the property is being marketed for sale or rent.

## Why ordinary coverage may not fit

Insurance pricing and eligibility depend partly on how a property is used and monitored. When nobody lives in a home, a small leak can continue for days, damage may not be reported promptly, and unauthorized entry may be harder to detect.

A vacant-home policy or endorsement may address eligible property damage and premises liability during the vacancy period. Available protections differ by insurer and may include special deductibles, inspection requirements, security conditions, or restrictions on certain causes of loss.

Coverage is not automatic. The application, policy form, endorsements, and exclusions determine what is insured.

## Renovation creates additional questions

Vacancy and renovation are related but not identical risks. Cosmetic work may be treated differently from structural work, a major addition, or a project that requires the building to be unoccupied.

Before work begins, be prepared to discuss:

- The complete scope and estimated cost of the project.
- Expected start and completion dates.
- Whether the structure, roof, plumbing, electrical, or HVAC systems will change.
- Contractor licensing, insurance, and use of subcontractors.
- Whether permits are required.
- How the site will be secured and inspected.

Depending on the project, vacant-property coverage, a renovation endorsement, or builders risk insurance may be considered. The contractor's insurance does not automatically replace the owner's need for property and liability protection.

## Information insurers commonly request

Gathering accurate details early can make the review more efficient:

1. Property address, construction type, year built, and number of units.
2. Current condition and recent updates to the roof and major systems.
3. Date the property became—or is expected to become—vacant.
4. Reason for the vacancy and expected duration.
5. Utility status, winterization, alarm systems, cameras, and locks.
6. Frequency of property inspections and who performs them.
7. Renovation plans, contractor information, or listing details.
8. Prior insurance and loss history.

Do not estimate an answer when the correct information can be confirmed. An accurate description helps the agent identify markets whose rules fit the actual property.

## Practical risk-reduction steps

An insurance policy is only part of a vacancy plan. Property owners should consider regular documented inspections, working smoke and security alarms, temperature monitoring, water-leak detection, mail collection, exterior lighting, and prompt maintenance.

Avoid turning off utilities without considering freezing, sump pumps, alarms, or other systems that depend on power or water. Ask the insurer whether it has specific protective-device or inspection requirements.

## Plan before the occupancy changes

The best time to discuss vacancy is before the last occupant leaves or major work starts. That gives the owner and agent time to review the current policy, identify possible restrictions, and compare available alternatives.
