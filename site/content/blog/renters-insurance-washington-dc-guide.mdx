Renters insurance is easy to overlook because you do not own the building. But the landlord's policy generally protects the building and the landlord's interests—not your furniture, clothing, electronics, or personal liability.

This guide explains the main parts of renters coverage and the questions Washington, DC tenants should ask before buying a policy. Policy language, limits, and exclusions control every claim, so use this as a starting point for a conversation with a licensed agent.

## What renters insurance generally covers

A renters policy commonly combines several protections:

- **Personal property coverage** may help repair or replace belongings damaged by a covered event such as fire, smoke, or theft.
- **Personal liability coverage** may help if you are legally responsible for another person's injury or property damage.
- **Additional living expenses** may help with eligible extra costs when a covered loss makes your rental temporarily uninhabitable.
- **Medical payments coverage** may provide limited payment for an eligible guest injury, regardless of fault, subject to the policy.

The exact covered events, exclusions, deductibles, and limits vary. Flood and earthquake damage are commonly handled separately, and high-value items may have special limits.

## How much personal property coverage do you need?

Walk through every room and estimate what it would cost to replace your belongings today. Include items that are easy to forget: kitchenware, shoes, linens, tools, sports equipment, bicycles, and small electronics.

A home inventory makes this easier. Take photos or video, record serial numbers for major items, and keep receipts when possible. Store the inventory somewhere you can access even if you cannot enter your home after a loss.

Ask whether the policy settles covered personal-property losses using **replacement cost** or **actual cash value**. Replacement-cost coverage generally considers the cost of a new comparable item, while actual cash value may account for age and depreciation.

## DC rental situations worth discussing

Washington renters live in many types of housing, including basement apartments, rowhouses, group homes, condominiums, and high-rise buildings. Tell your agent about the actual arrangement.

Roommates generally need their own policies unless an insurer has specifically agreed to include them. If you work from home, standard renters coverage may place limits on business equipment or business liability. Jewelry, art, musical instruments, collectibles, and expensive bicycles may also require additional scheduling or endorsements.

## What affects the price?

Pricing can depend on the address, building characteristics, requested limits, deductible, prior claims, protective devices, and optional coverages. A low premium is useful only when the policy still protects the risks that matter to you.

Compare the deductible and coverage terms—not only the monthly price. Also ask whether coordinating renters and auto policies creates a useful discount, but compare the combined value rather than assuming a bundle is automatically best.

## Before you choose a policy

Have these details ready:

1. Your address and move-in date.
2. An estimate of the replacement cost of your belongings.
3. Any high-value property that may need special treatment.
4. Your preferred liability limit and deductible.
5. Information about pets, roommates, home-based work, or other exposures.
